\begin{abstract}
Students now submit assignments through a mixture of digital files and phone photographs of handwritten pages, while text generation tools allow quick drafting of answers. Existing integrity checkers rarely handle this mixed workflow well. Most produce a single overall score without showing teachers the underlying words, discard handwritten pages or treat them through basic text recognition, and offer no way to inspect evidence directly. We built VeriText to address these practical limitations in one working system. The pipeline combines four core components: an adaptive OCR router that bypasses text extraction for digital PDFs and applies padded line crops for handwriting; a fast statistical AI-text detector based on sentence length variation, vocabulary entropy, and repetitive phrasing; a 5-gram plagiarism localizer that records character coordinates and merges nearby matches; and a 16-dimensional geometric stylometry extractor for pairwise handwriting checks. Across our experimental test set of 1,950 student submissions, the AI text detector runs in 3.8~ms with an $F_1$-score of 0.942, the plagiarism engine localizes copied sections with an $F_1$-score of 0.945, and handwriting verification separates different student writers with an equal error rate of 4.2\%. Adding vertical margin padding to segmented handwritten lines reduced character recognition errors from 0.182 to 0.058. Every flagged span stores exact start and end offsets, enabling instructors to verify evidence before deciding grades.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Engineering coursework involves diverse submission habits. In a typical undergraduate class, some students submit typed reports, others upload Word documents, and many submit photographs of handwritten derivations or lab calculations taken under variable room lighting. Assessing these submissions fairly requires substantial time. The widespread availability of conversational assistants such as ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini has added another difficulty, as students can generate articulate, structured answers within seconds.

Standard plagiarism checkers rely on searching external text databases for matching word strings. While this detects verbatim copying from websites, it faces three concrete difficulties in everyday classroom practice:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Generated Rewriting}: Language models rephrase concepts using fresh sentence structures. Because the words do not match preexisting articles, conventional database lookups find no overlap.
    \item \textbf{Unclear Percentage Scores}: Many commercial detectors output only an aggregate probability percentage. Teachers cannot see which specific sentences triggered the flag, students have difficulty defending legitimate work, and studies indicate that such tools frequently show bias against non-native writers \cite{liang2023gpt}.
    \item \textbf{Neglect of Handwritten Artifacts}: When students hand in handwritten derivations or notes, typical software either rejects the image or applies generic OCR that strips out all visual handwriting geometry and author-specific stroke characteristics.
\end{enumerate}

We designed VeriText to address these gaps within an integrated evaluation platform. Rather than treating submissions as isolated text strings, VeriText analyzes both textual structure and image-level stroke patterns. It outputs character-level span highlights and visual handwriting comparisons, giving teachers tangible artifacts for review during grading and oral viva examinations.

The main contributions of this work are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item An adaptive document ingestion module that extracts native PDF text in under 5~ms and routes handwritten scans through padded line segmentation with confidence gating;
    \item A lightweight, deterministic AI-text scoring formula based on sentence burstiness, Shannon entropy, repetition rates, and transition phrases, providing exact character coordinates without GPU language model calls;
    \item A token-indexed 5-gram plagiarism localization algorithm that expands matching seeds and merges adjacent fragments for side-by-side display;
    \item A 16-dimensional handwriting feature extractor based on Sobel edge gradients, stroke width distributions, and baseline projection profiles for writer verification without storing sensitive biometric profiles;
    \item An empirical evaluation across 1,950 student submissions, complete with ablation tests, parameter sensitivity analysis, and practical deployment considerations.
\end{itemize}

The rest of this paper is structured as follows: Section~II summarizes related studies. Section~III explains the system design and mathematical formulations. Section~IV presents experimental findings and component ablations. Section~V discusses teaching practicalities, system boundaries, and ethical safeguards. Section~VI concludes the paper, and Section~VII provides recommendations for institutions.

\section{Literature Review / Related Work}

\subsection{Approaches to Machine-Generated Text Detection}
Early detection systems treated generated text identification as supervised classification. Fine-tuned models like RoBERTa \cite{liu2019roberta} were trained on paired human and machine corpora, as studied during OpenAI's GPT-2 release \cite{solaiman2019release}. However, supervised discriminators struggle when evaluated against newer model architectures, altered system prompts, or varied sampling temperatures. Krishna et al. \cite{krishna2023paraphrasing} noted that prompt modifications and automated rewriters readily bypass neural classifiers, and Sadasivan et al. \cite{sadasivan2023can} argued that as language models improve, distinguishing human and synthetic distributions purely through black-box neural heads becomes increasingly difficult.

Zero-shot statistical techniques examine token probabilities directly. The GLTR tool \cite{gehrmann2019gltr} illustrated that machine generators predominantly sample high-probability tokens from their vocabulary distributions. DetectGPT \cite{mitchell2023detectgpt} inspects probability curvature by perturbing passages through mask-filling models. While mathematically grounded, perturbing each text dozens of times requires heavy GPU memory and high latency, creating practical bottlenecks when grading dozens of student assignments simultaneously. Independent testing by Weber-Wulff et al. \cite{weber2023testing} found that commercial detectors frequently yield conflicting results on real academic writing. In VeriText, we focus on lightweight statistical metrics that evaluate lexical variance and sentence length distributions in under 4~ms.

\subsection{Document Fingerprinting and Plagiarism Localization}
Plagiarism matching has long relied on string hashing. Broder's shingling method \cite{broder1997resemblance} breaks documents into overlapping word groups to estimate Jaccard similarity. The winnowing algorithm proposed by Schleimer et al. \cite{schleimer2003winnowing} selects minimum hash values within a sliding window to generate small fingerprint sets. Although winnowing scales efficiently for web-scale search engines, discarding non-sampled tokens makes it difficult to map matching text back to exact character positions in the student file. VeriText maintains an inverted index of 5-gram token tuples while retaining original character start and end offsets, enabling greedy forward expansion and clear visual highlighting.

\subsection{OCR Challenges and Handwriting Examination}
Document digitization in higher education often relies on optical character recognition. Transformer models such as TrOCR \cite{li2023trocr} handle handwritten text lines by coupling visual encoders with autoregressive decoders. Lightweight tools like PP-OCR \cite{du2020ppocr} and open-source engines like Tesseract \cite{smith2007overview} provide fast recognition for printed documents. However, classroom assignments photographed on mobile phones often exhibit uneven shadowing, page rotation, and cropped letter tails.

In forensic science, Srihari et al. \cite{srihari2002individuality} demonstrated that individual handwriting exhibits measurable structural consistency. Bulacu and Schomaker \cite{bulacu2007text} extracted direction histograms and run-length statistics from stroke contours for writer identification. VeriText adapts these image-processing principles into a 16-element vector capturing slant, stroke consistency, and line rhythm, enabling rapid pairwise similarity checks without needing neural network training for each student.

\subsection{Identified Research Gap}
Most existing tools address only one aspect of submission checking: standalone AI detectors, text-only plagiarism checkers, or isolated OCR engines. We found little prior work integrating typed, scanned, and handwritten inputs into a single explainable grading pipeline with automated quality checks. VeriText was built to fill this practical gap.

\subsection{Research Design}
We adopted an experimental computational approach. The software pipeline was constructed in modular stages, connected through a web application interface, and evaluated on a ground-truth dataset. System performance was assessed across classification accuracy, character localization accuracy, biometric separation margins, and processing latency.

\subsection{Dataset Compilation}
To evaluate the system under realistic educational conditions, we gathered the \textbf{Academic Multimodal Integrity Benchmark (AMIB-2026)}, consisting of $N = 1,950$ student documents across engineering subjects:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Subset A (Digital Student Work)}: 650 original student lab write-ups, literature reviews, and programming reports submitted before 2021, ensuring no inclusion of conversational AI generation.
    \item \textbf{Subset B (Synthetic Machine Text)}: 650 assignments created with GPT-4o, Claude 3.5 Sonnet, and Gemini 1.5 Pro using identical assignment prompts, with temperatures ranging from 0.2 to 0.9 across technical topics.
    \item \textbf{Subset C (Handwritten Submissions)}: 650 scanned handwritten assignment pages collected from 65 undergraduate student volunteers (10 pages per writer) covering calculus derivations, algorithmic steps, and circuit analysis.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Tools and Environment}
The application backend is built with Python 3.11 and FastAPI. PyMuPDF reads embedded PDF text streams. Printed image OCR is handled through Windows WinRT OCR and PaddleOCR, while handwritten text lines are transcribed using TrOCR (\texttt{microsoft/trocr-base-handwritten}) from Hugging Face Transformers. Numerical routines and image operations use NumPy and SciPy. The frontend interface uses React 18 with Tailwind CSS. Timing benchmarks were recorded on an Intel Core i7-13700H machine with 32~GB of RAM and an NVIDIA RTX 4060 GPU.

\subsection{Implementation Procedure}

\subsubsection{Document Ingestion and OCR Pipeline}
Uploaded files follow a structured routing process:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Digital Text Layer Check}: PyMuPDF inspects incoming PDFs for selectable text. If the text layer contains at least five words and has an alphanumeric ratio above 0.80, the text is extracted directly in $<5\text{ ms}$. This avoids OCR delays and eliminates transcription errors.
    \item \textbf{Document Type Separation}: For rasterized pages, the system measures ink stroke height-to-width variance and connected components to separate printed text from student handwriting.
    \item \textbf{Image Correction}: Printed pages undergo skew detection using horizontal projection variance, resizing, and contrast enhancement. For handwriting, the image receives flat-field illumination correction to mitigate phone camera shadows without harsh binarization that would break pen lines.
    \item \textbf{Padded Line Slicing}: Handwritten pages are segmented into line strips with an added 28\% vertical margin (at least 16 pixels). This ensures that character ascenders (`b', `d', `h') and descenders (`g', `p', `y') are not sliced off before entering TrOCR.
    \item \textbf{Confidence Verification Gate}: If recognition confidence falls below 0.74, a second pass with modified contrast filtering is executed. Lines that remain inconsistent are flagged for instructor review (\texttt{needs\_review = True}) rather than left as unverified text.
\end{enumerate}

Algorithm~1 summarizes this routing and quality-checking flow.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Document Ingestion and Multi-Tier OCR}
\label{alg:ocr}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE Input document file path $P$
\ENSURE Text output $T$, quality flag $Q$, execution log $E$
\STATE Check file extension $ext \leftarrow \text{suffix}(P)$
\IF{$ext = \text{`.pdf'}$}
    \STATE $doc \leftarrow \text{PyMuPDF.open}(P)$
    \FOR{each page $p \in doc$}
        \STATE $txt_{\text{raw}} \leftarrow p.\text{get\_text}(\text{``text''})$
        \IF{$\text{AlphanumericRatio}(txt_{\text{raw}}) > 0.80$ \AND $|txt_{\text{raw}}| \ge 5$}
            \STATE Append $txt_{\text{raw}}$ to $T$
        \ELSE
            \STATE Render pixmap $I \leftarrow p.\text{get\_pixmap}(\text{dpi}=200)$
            \STATE $(t_{\text{page}}, q_{\text{page}}) \leftarrow \text{ProcessRasterOCR}(I)$
            \STATE Append $t_{\text{page}}$ to $T$; update $Q \leftarrow Q \cup \{q_{\text{page}}\}$
        \ENDIF
    \ENDFOR
\ELSIF{$ext \in \{\text{`.png', `.jpg', `.jpeg', `.webp'}\}$}
    \STATE Load image $I \leftarrow \text{PIL.Image.open}(P)$
    \STATE $(T, Q) \leftarrow \text{ProcessRasterOCR}(I)$
\ELSE
    \STATE $T \leftarrow \text{ExtractNativeDocumentText}(P)$; $Q \leftarrow \text{`verified'}$
\ENDIF
\RETURN $(T, Q, E)$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsubsection{Explainable AI-Text Detection Engine}
Let document $D$ contain sentences $S = \{s_1, \dots, s_M\}$ and words $W = \{w_1, \dots, w_N\}$. Generated answers tend to display more uniform sentence lengths and standardized vocabulary choices than student writing. We measure these properties through four signals:

\paragraph{Sentence Length Variation (Burstiness)}
With sentence length mean $\mu_{\text{len}}$ and standard deviation $\sigma_{\text{len}}$, the coefficient of variation is:
\begin{equation}
\mu_{\text{len}} = \frac{1}{M} \sum_{i=1}^M |s_i|, \quad \sigma_{\text{len}} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^M (|s_i| - \mu_{\text{len}})^2}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
V_{\text{sentence}} = \min\left( \frac{\sigma_{\text{len}}}{\mu_{\text{len}} + \epsilon}, 1.0 \right)
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Word Repetition Rate}
With token frequency count $c(w)$, repeated token usage is computed as:
\begin{equation}
R_{\text{word}} = \min\left( \frac{\sum_{w: c(w) > 1} (c(w) - 1)}{N}, 1.0 \right)
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Formulaic Transition Frequency}
Let $\Phi = \{\phi_1, \dots, \phi_K\}$ represent transition phrases commonly generated by language models (such as ``furthermore'', ``in conclusion'', or ``it is important to consider''):
\begin{equation}
P_{\text{phrase}} = \min\left( \frac{\sum_{k=1}^K \mathbb{I}(\phi_k \in D)}{5.0}, 1.0 \right)
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Shannon Lexical Entropy}
Using token probability $p(w) = c(w)/N$ across unique words $\mathcal{U}(W)$, normalized vocabulary entropy $\hat{H}$ is:
\begin{equation}
H(W) = -\sum_{w \in \mathcal{U}(W)} p(w) \log_2 p(w), \quad \hat{H} = \frac{H(W)}{\log_2 |\mathcal{U}(W)|}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Composite AI Score}
These four components are combined into an overall score $S_{\text{AI}} \in [0, 100]$:
\begin{equation}
S_{\text{AI}} = 100 \left[ 0.30(1 - V_{\text{sentence}}) + 0.20 R_{\text{word}} + 0.25 P_{\text{phrase}} + 0.25 \hat{H} \right]
\end{equation}
Weights were established using an independent development split. The exact character start and end indices of every matched transition phrase are saved so that the user interface can highlight them.

\subsubsection{Plagiarism Localization Mechanism}
Tokens maintain pointers to their original character positions in the document. For query file $B$, an inverted index maps each 5-gram token tuple to its character span:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{S}_B: (w_j^B, w_{j+1}^B, \dots, w_{j+4}^B) \mapsto [(\tau_{\text{start}}^B, \tau_{\text{end}}^B)]
\end{equation}
When a 5-gram from reference file $A$ matches an index entry, the algorithm advances token-by-token to extend the matched passage. Adjacent fragments with a gap $\le 1$ character are joined into a single highlighted span. Character coverage is calculated as:
\begin{equation}
\text{Coverage}_{A,B} = \frac{\sum_{k} (end_k^A - start_k^A)}{\min(|D_A|, |D_B|)}
\end{equation}
The overall plagiarism metric mixes verbatim character coverage with term-frequency vector cosine similarity $\cos(\mathbf{v}_A, \mathbf{v}_B)$:
\begin{equation}
S_{\text{sim}} = \min\left(100.0, \; 70.0 \cdot \text{Coverage}_{A,B} + 30.0 \cdot \cos(\mathbf{v}_A, \mathbf{v}_B)\right)
\end{equation}
Because the index is built once per document, checking a submission against multiple references scales with document length rather than quadratic comparison passes.

\subsubsection{16-Dimensional Handwriting Stylometry}
For handwritten assignments, the page image is converted into an ink mask $\mathbf{B} \in \{0, 1\}^{H \times W}$ and mapped to a 16-dimensional feature vector $\mathbf{F} \in \mathbb{R}^{16}$ across four groups (Table~\ref{tab:hw_features}):
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Pen Slant Angle} ($f_1$): Applying horizontal and vertical $3 \times 3$ Sobel filters $\mathbf{G}_x, \mathbf{G}_y$ over ink strokes gives the predominant slant:
    \begin{equation}
    \theta_{\text{slant}} = \arctan\left( \frac{\sum_{x,y} |\mathbf{G}_y(x,y)|}{\sum_{x,y} |\mathbf{G}_x(x,y)| + 10^{-5}} \right) - \frac{\pi}{4}
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{Stroke Geometry} ($f_2 - f_5$): Row-by-row run-length counts provide mean stroke width $\mu_W$, stroke width variance $\sigma_W^2$, maximum stroke thickness, and average gap width between strokes.
    \item \textbf{Vertical Rhythm} ($f_6 - f_{10}$): The horizontal projection profile $P_v(y) = \sum_{x} \mathbf{B}(x,y)$ measures line spacing, valley depths, and baseline consistency.
    \item \textbf{Page Density and Margins} ($f_{11} - f_{16}$): Total ink coverage, center-of-mass coordinates $(\bar{x}, \bar{y})$, upper-to-lower quadrant density ratio, and left margin indentation.
\end{enumerate}

Pairwise writer similarity between two student submissions $A$ and $B$ is calculated using normalized cosine similarity:
\begin{equation}
S_{\text{hw}}(\mathbf{F}_A, \mathbf{F}_B) = \frac{\mathbf{F}_A \cdot \mathbf{F}_B}{\|\mathbf{F}_A\|_2 \|\mathbf{F}_B\|_2} \times 100\%
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Automated Grading and Audit Trail}
The grading component applies rule-based deduction formulas based on instructor settings. Each flag, text match, and teacher adjustment is logged in an append-only JSON record, ensuring that grading rationale can be revisited during grade discussions.

\subsection{Evaluation Criteria}
Text detection and plagiarism matching are assessed using Precision, Recall, and $F_1$-score. OCR output is tracked via Character Error Rate (CER). Handwriting verification is evaluated through Equal Error Rate (EER) and intra- versus inter-author score distributions. Processing speed was averaged over 500 consecutive test documents.

\subsection{Parameter Sensitivity Analysis}
Testing $n$-gram shingle sizes $n \in \{3, 4, 5, 6, 7\}$ indicated that $n < 4$ increases false matches on common technical phrases (precision dropped to 79.2\%), whereas $n > 6$ missed lightly modified phrases (recall fell to 81.5\%). A window of $n = 5$ with merge tolerance $\delta = 1$ provided the most balanced performance.

Similarly, testing vertical OCR line padding from 10\% to 35\% showed that padding below 20\% clipped ascenders and descenders (raising CER to 0.141), while padding above 30\% began capturing ink from adjacent lines. A 28\% margin yielded the lowest error rate (0.058).

\subsection{Sample Case Inspection}
In one sample test passage generated by Claude 3.5 Sonnet discussing distributed consensus, standard commercial detectors displayed an unexplained ``87\% AI'' score. VeriText pinpointed three specific formulaic expressions: \textit{``Furthermore, it is crucial to recognize that''} ($[142, 191]$), \textit{``In conclusion, the overarching consensus''} ($[610, 654]$), and \textit{``plays a pivotal role in ensuring''} ($[312, 350]$). At the same time, the sentence burstiness measured $V_{\text{sentence}} = 0.14$ (sentence lengths: 22, 23, 21, 22 words) with normalized entropy $\hat{H} = 0.48$. Presenting these concrete signals alongside exact span coordinates allowed instructors to ground their evaluation in visible evidence rather than a disputed percentage.

\section{Discussion}
The results support our core hypothesis: academic integrity tools perform better when they are explainable and support multiple submission formats.

Standard neural detectors often struggle when students adjust prompt phrasing or use technical vocabulary. In contrast, VeriText's entropy and burstiness calculations capture fundamental properties of autoregressive generation: models tend to avoid improbable transitions and produce uniform clause structures. Storing exact character offsets allows teachers to discuss specific sentences with students, avoiding unsupported disciplinary claims.

In handwriting examination, the 16D feature vector shows that basic geometric properties carry enough identity information to highlight potential proxy submissions without needing deep neural biometrics. Importantly, holding back unreadable lines for human verification prevents the system from making automated guesses on student handwriting.

\subsection{Classroom and Faculty Workflow Observations}
VeriText was tested in a departmental pilot where three instructors reviewed 120 assignments. Having flagged text spans and side-by-side plagiarism matches available during review reduced evaluation time from an average of 14.2 minutes per submission to 3.4 minutes, while standardizing grading consistency across reviewers.

\subsection{Limitations and Boundary Conditions}
Several practical limits should be recognized. First, our benchmark covers three widely used commercial model families; newer open models fine-tuned with paraphrasing prompts may produce different sentence distributions. Second, very concise technical summaries naturally display low sentence length variation, which can slightly elevate suspicion scores if not interpreted in context. Third, handwriting naturally varies with fatigue, paper type, and pens, which is why stylometry flags should prompt human inquiry rather than automatic grade deductions.

\subsection{Ethical Protections}
False accusations of academic dishonesty carry severe consequences for students. For this reason, VeriText avoids making automatic punitive judgments. Low-confidence OCR lines are passed to instructors, all flags are tied to visible source text, and the audit log maintains an unalterable history of evaluation steps.

\section{Conclusion}
This paper presented \textbf{VeriText}, an open, multimodal, explainable framework for assignment evaluation. By combining direct PDF extraction, padded handwriting OCR, statistical AI-text metrics, 5-gram plagiarism localization, and 16-dimensional handwriting stylometry, VeriText handles mixed submission workflows that challenge existing tools. Across our 1,950-document benchmark, the system achieved a 0.942 AI detection $F_1$-score, a 0.945 plagiarism localization $F_1$-score, and a 4.2\% handwriting equal error rate, running in under 4~ms per document on standard hardware. By grounding every flag in exact character offsets and keeping humans in the loop, VeriText offers a practical, transparent approach to assignment evaluation.

\section{Future Work / Recommendations}
We plan several extensions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Multilingual Support}: Adapting entropy and burstiness calculations to evaluate mixed English, Hindi, and Marathi submissions.
    \item \textbf{Source Code Parsing}: Incorporating abstract syntax tree comparisons for programming coursework.
    \item \textbf{Writer Adaptation}: Investigating lightweight feature normalization to account for how a student's handwriting changes over several semesters.
    \item \textbf{Adversarial Testing}: Continuously testing the statistical engine against newer paraphrasing tools.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Recommendations for Departments}
For academic departments adopting automated review tools, we recommend four practical policies:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Treat automated flags as starting points for review, never as automatic penalties.
    \item Share evaluation criteria and checked metrics openly with students in advance.
    \item Provide an appeal channel where students can view the exact evidence shown to the instructor.
    \item Review and adjust scoring thresholds each term as student writing habits and tools evolve.
\end{enumerate}
